\section*{Introduction}
\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

An algorithm with favourable retrospective performance is only one part of a clinical
product. To be used in a screening programme, a grading system would also have to be
built as medical-device software: its components must be verifiable, its risks analysed,
its decisions traceable and explainable, and it must integrate with the hospital
information system. In Europe, these requirements are set by the IEC~62304 and ISO~14971
standards and by the EU AI Act, which classifies AI used in medical devices as high-risk.

This chapter describes the software architecture of the pipelines developed in this
thesis -- the six-stage TOPORET framework~\cite{belhadj2026enase} and the DGTS and DOTS
pipelines of \cref{ch:dots} -- and how regulatory-oriented principles guided it. We first
map selected requirements of each standard and article to the architectural mechanism
relevant to it, then present the verification strategy, the risk analysis, the
computational cost, the integration with electronic health records and a three-tier
deployment architecture. We end with the audit trail and explanation template, the
ethical considerations, and an explicit statement of what this design approach does not
solve.

\section{Regulatory-Oriented Design Principles}
\label{sec:regprinciple}

The pipelines were designed with regulatory-oriented principles relevant to
IEC~62304~\cite{iec62304}, ISO~14971~\cite{iso14971} and the applicable requirements of
Articles~9--15 of the EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} in mind. The mapping presented in this
chapter documents how selected architectural mechanisms address requirements relevant to
software life-cycle management, risk management, traceability, human oversight and
transparency.

This mapping should not be interpreted as formal regulatory compliance, conformity
assessment, clinical evaluation, CE marking, FDA clearance or notified-body approval.
Those activities require evidence and processes beyond the scope of the present doctoral
work (\cref{sec:regnotsolve}).

Research prototypes of DR grading are often built to maximise accuracy first, with
regulatory questions addressed afterwards. A system designed in that way may need
architectural changes when a gap is discovered late: for example, when no component can
produce a per-prediction explanation, a separate post-hoc module (Grad-CAM, SHAP,
LIME~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam,lundberg2017shap,ribeiro2016lime}) has to be added, and
its explanations may not faithfully represent the model's decision. The pipelines of this
thesis instead expose, at inference time, quantities that can be shown to a clinician
without a separate model. TOPORET provides the topological descriptors of the vessel
network; DGTS provides the most similar training cases with their verified grades and the
persistence diagram of their neighbourhood~\cite{belhadj2026dgts}; DOTS provides
calibrated grade probabilities~\cite{belhadj2026dots}. These quantities are explanatory signals, not demonstrated
faithful explanations; their usefulness to clinicians has not been evaluated.

\subsection{IEC 62304: Software Life Cycle and Unit Verification}
\label{sec:iec62304}

IEC~62304 classifies medical-device software into three safety classes (A, B, C)
according to the severity of harm that a software failure could cause. A decision-support
system whose output informs referral decisions is treated here as Class~B (non-serious
injury possible) under a conservative reading, pending formal classification by a
notified body.

Class~B requires documented unit verification. The TOPORET framework is organised in six
stages with typed interfaces (\cref{tab:stages})~\cite{belhadj2026enase}: each stage has an
explicit input and output contract (for example, stage S3 accepts the skeleton of a
vessel map and returns a topological vector of fixed length), so that it can be tested,
replaced and audited in isolation. \Cref{tab:unittests} gives the verification plan
associated with each stage; it lists what must be checked at each boundary, and is a
design document rather than a report of test results.

six-stage framework (IEC~62304 unit verification).}
\label{tab:unittests}

For the CORAL head of DOTS, the last check is a property rather than an input--output
example: for random inputs, the cumulative probabilities $\hat P(Y\geq1)\geq\hat
P(Y\geq2)\geq\cdots$ must decrease. With the ordered biases of \cref{eq:coral-proj} this
holds for every input, so the check verifies the implementation of the constraint
independently of the trained weights.

\paragraph{Residual risks.}
Hazard~5 is mitigated but not eliminated: the measured Sev-NR of DOTS is $0.97\%$
internally and $1.4\%$ on Messidor-2, and neither pipeline defers uncertain images to a
human grader. Hazard~6 has no implemented mitigation. Both residual risks are acceptable
for the retrospective evaluation of this thesis, but not for clinical deployment; they are
the basis of the phased roadmap of \cref{sec:roadmap}.

\paragraph{Severity definitions.}
\emph{Critical}: potential for irreversible patient harm (missed sight-threatening
diagnosis). \emph{Major}: potential for delayed but recoverable harm, or systematic
accuracy loss requiring intervention. \emph{Moderate}: workflow disruption without direct
patient-safety implications.
The three critical hazards are those whose effect reaches the patient directly; two of
them (hazards~1 and~5) are mitigated inside the pipeline, whereas hazard~6 depends on the
integration of the pipeline into the hospital information system.

\section{EU AI Act: High-Risk Classification}
\label{sec:aiact}

Under Article~6(1) of the EU AI Act, an AI system that is itself a medical device, or a
safety component of one, is high-risk when the device requires a third-party conformity
assessment under the Medical Device Regulation (EU)~2017/745, listed in Annex~I of the
Act; a DR grading system that informs referral decisions falls in this case, since
Rule~11 of the Regulation places such software in Class~IIa or higher. This
triggers the requirements of Articles~9--15, assessed together with the CE marking of the
device. The design includes mechanisms relevant to each of them; whether they are
sufficient would be decided only by a formal conformity assessment.
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.2em]
\item[Art.~9 (risk management)] The FMEA of \cref{tab:fmea}, with explicit failure
handling at stage boundaries.
\item[Art.~10 (data governance)] Public datasets with documented provenance, licence
terms and known limitations (\cref{app:datasets}); stratified folds with a fixed seed;
perceptual-hash check of the absence of duplicates between training and external data for
DGTS.
\item[Art.~11--12 (technical documentation, logging)] Versioned configurations of the
experiments (\cref{app:repro}) and the structured prediction log of \cref{sec:audit}.
\item[Art.~13 (transparency)] Similar training cases with verified grades, the
persistence diagram of their neighbourhood, topological descriptors and calibrated
probabilities, all computed in the inference path; their faithfulness as explanations has
not been evaluated.
\item[Art.~14 (human oversight)] The pipelines are decision-support tools: the grade is
shown with its calibrated confidence and the similar cases, and the referral decision
remains with the clinician. An automatic deferral of uncertain images is not implemented.
\item[Art.~15 (accuracy, robustness)] Cross-validation, external validation on EyePACS
and Messidor-2, and calibration analysis (\cref{ch:dots}).
\end{description}

Persistent homology is the main cost of the TOPORET framework; it is independent for each
image and parallelises across CPU cores, and in DGTS it scales almost linearly with the
number of workers~\cite{belhadj2026dgts}. At $75$~ms per image, DOTS processes about
$48{,}000$ images per hour on one T4 GPU, while a programme screening $500{,}000$ patients a
year needs about $1{,}370$ per day. These measurements indicate computational feasibility
at the laboratory level; operational feasibility also depends on image acquisition,
network infrastructure, integration with information systems (\cref{sec:fhir}), quality
control and human review.

\paragraph{Memory footprint.}
DGTS keeps a cached index of the training features ($\approx33$~MB for the fused corpus)
and uses at most $4.2$~GB of GPU memory at inference~\cite{belhadj2026dgts}. New reference
images can be added to the index without retraining the backbone.

\section{FHIR R4 Integration Gap}
\label{sec:fhir}

FHIR R4~\cite{hl7fhir} is the international standard for exchanging electronic health
record (EHR) data. Without a FHIR adapter, the outputs of the pipelines cannot be written
automatically to the patient record and would require manual transcription -- a workflow
friction that would likely deter routine clinical adoption regardless of accuracy. Full
integration requires three components.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Output mapping.} The output (grade, calibrated probability vector, similar
cases) is mapped to a FHIR \texttt{Observation} resource using the appropriate SNOMED~CT
concept for each ICDR grade.
\item \textbf{Input ingestion.} A DICOM adapter extracts the fundus image and its metadata
(pseudonymised patient identifier, device, timestamp) from the ophthalmic imaging
workflow.
\item \textbf{Point-of-care embedding.} CDS Hooks integration lets predictions appear
inside the clinician's existing EHR interface rather than in a separate application.
\end{enumerate}
The FHIR adapter is not implemented; it is the largest gap between the retrospectively
evaluated research pipelines and a clinical product.

\section{Audit Trail and Article 13 Explanation Template}
\label{sec:audit}

The design specifies that every prediction be logged with the patient pseudonym, the
image DICOM UID, a timestamp, the software version, the full five-grade probability vector
after temperature scaling and, for the graph-based pipelines, the identifiers and grades of
the retrieved similar cases. The retention period would follow national rules for medical
records, in line with the storage-limitation principle of GDPR Article~5(1)(e)~\cite{gdpr2016}.

An Article~13 explanation can be generated from these logged quantities without a
separate explanation model:

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
Grade [$k$] predicted with a calibrated probability of [$p$]\%. The eight most similar
reference images had grades [$g_1,\ldots,g_8$]. The neighbourhood of this image is
[tight / diffuse] according to its persistence diagram.
\end{quote}

The template reports only quantities computed during inference. It avoids the specific
risk that a separate post-hoc model misrepresents the grading model, but it does not
establish that the similar cases or the persistence diagram faithfully reflect the
decision; evaluating their usefulness with clinicians is left to the reader study of
Phase~2.

\subsection{Future Extension: Evidence-Grounded LLM Explanations}
\label{sec:llm}

The fixed template is deliberately simple. A natural extension, not implemented in this
thesis, is to turn the logged quantities into an evidence-grounded narrative adapted to
its reader, clinician or patient. \Cref{fig:llm} presents the architecture proposed for
this purpose. It is organised in eight modules.

\input{figures/fig_llm}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Input layer.} The fundus image and the clinical data of the patient (age,
sex, diabetes duration, HbA1c, blood pressure, comorbidities).
\item \textbf{Diagnosis engine.} The grading pipeline produces the grade, its calibrated
confidence, the similar cases and the image embeddings.
\item \textbf{Retrieval module.} A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) component queries a
vector database of grading criteria, clinical guidelines, textbooks and research papers,
and returns the $k$ most relevant passages for the clinical question.
\item \textbf{Clinical reasoning orchestrator.} A graph is built from the embeddings, the
prediction, the clinical features and the quality of the retrieved evidence; a graph
network with a scoring layer selects the most suitable reasoning strategy among a
library of prompts: strict grounding, evidence first, uncertainty-aware, guideline
comparison, clinician explanation or patient explanation.
\item \textbf{LLM reasoning module.} A large language model uses the selected prompt and
the retrieved evidence to write the explanation and a management plan.
\item \textbf{Output layer.} Grade, key findings, supporting references, management plan,
confidence and uncertainty, phrased for the clinician or for the patient.
\item \textbf{Experience memory.} Every case is stored with its embeddings, evidence,
selected strategy, outcome and feedback, so that similar past cases can guide future
selections.
\item \textbf{Feedback and continuous learning.} Clinician feedback on each explanation is
used to update the selection policy and the memory.
\end{enumerate}

The orchestrator reuses the idea at the heart of this thesis: just as the population graph
uses similar patients to grade a new one, the orchestrator uses similar past cases to
choose how to explain it. For example, when the calibrated confidence is low, the
uncertainty-aware strategy would state explicitly that the case is ambiguous and should be
reviewed, rather than presenting a confident narrative.

Such an extension raises questions outside the scope of this thesis, chiefly how to
prevent a fluent narrative from overriding or contradicting the grade and its confidence.
No LLM experiments were conducted; this subsection is a scoped direction for future work.
Should such a layer be built, it would need its own evaluation criteria, distinct from
those of the grading model: \emph{faithfulness} (every statement must be traceable to a
logged quantity), \emph{consistency} (the narrative must never contradict the grade or the
confidence), and \emph{usefulness}, measured with clinicians and patients in the reader
study of Phase~2.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

Three ethical considerations go beyond formal regulatory requirements.

\paragraph{Algorithmic fairness across demographic subgroups.}
The datasets used in this thesis come from several countries, which gives some
geographic and probably some ethnic diversity. None of them, however, provides per-image
demographic metadata (age, sex, ethnicity), which prevents a formal subgroup fairness
analysis. This is stated as a limitation in the General Conclusion rather than elided.

\paragraph{Deskilling risk.}
Reliance on automated screening may, over time, erode human grading skill. Showing the
similar reference cases with their grades keeps the grader engaged with the evidence,
and routing low-confidence cases to human review -- a mechanism not implemented here --
would keep graders practising on the most difficult images.

\paragraph{Accountability for missed referrals.}
Even at a Sev-NR of $1.4\%$ on external data, some severe cases would be missed. The
audit trail of \cref{sec:audit} makes every missed case traceable to a logged prediction,
which supports clinical-governance review. It does not settle the broader question of
legal and professional accountability when an automated system contributes to a missed
diagnosis. In all cases, the pipelines are designed as decision-support tools: the
responsibility for the final referral decision remains with the clinician.

\section{What Regulatory-Oriented Design Does Not Solve}
\label{sec:regnotsolve}

Designing with regulatory requirements in mind from the outset can avoid some late
architectural changes; it is worth being equally explicit about what it does not solve.
The mapping of \cref{tab:regmap} documents a regulatory-oriented \emph{architecture}: typed
stage interfaces, a risk analysis, a logging design and explanatory outputs of the kind that
a notified body would expect to examine. It does not constitute the conformity
assessment, clinical evaluation or notified-body review required before CE marking; those
remain later activities outside the scope of a doctoral thesis. The FHIR gap of
\cref{sec:fhir} is the concrete reason why the pipelines could not be connected to a live
clinical workflow today, and the absence of an automatic deferral mechanism leaves
Hazard~5 of \cref{tab:fmea} only partly mitigated. The results of
\cref{ch:toporet,ch:dots} constitute Level~1 (computational) evidence; regulatory clearance
(Level~4) requires prospective validation, notified-body assessment and post-market
surveillance, none of which is conducted here.

\section{Towards Clinical Deployment: Translation Roadmap}
\label{sec:roadmap}\label{app:roadmap}



A possible translation into clinical practice is outlined in four planned phases
(\cref{tab:roadmap}), following the four levels of evidence used in diagnostic AI
evaluation: Level~1 computational evidence, Level~2 prospective agreement with human
readers, Level~3 randomised patient outcomes, and Level~4 regulatory clearance. This
thesis provides Level~1 evidence only.



Phase~2 is the critical next step. It would combine the three axes in one model with an
automatic deferral of uncertain images, and evaluate it in a pre-registered, multi-site
reader study in which ophthalmologists grade images with and without the system. Its
primary success criterion would be an upper bound of the 95\% confidence interval of
Sev-NR below $2\%$, which would close the main statistical limitation of
the present results. Because Sev-NR concerns rare severe cases, the sample size would have
to be set by a power calculation on the number of severe cases, and is likely to be large.
The dates of Phases~2--4 are indicative.

Each phase would end with an explicit exit criterion, so that the next one starts only on
evidence. Phase~2 would close when the upper bound of the Sev-NR interval lies below $2\%$
and the FHIR adapter has passed integration testing at every site; Phase~3 when the
randomised trial shows that AI-assisted screening is not inferior to standard care for the
referral of sight-threatening disease; and Phase~4 when a notified body accepts the
technical documentation and the clinical evaluation.

\section*{Conclusion}
\phantomsection\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}

This chapter mapped the architecture of the pipelines to selected requirements of
IEC~62304, ISO~14971 and the EU AI Act (\cref{tab:regmap}), as a design aid rather than a
conformity assessment. The six-stage framework supports stage-level verification; the
FMEA leaves two residual risks, missed severe cases and the missing EHR integration.
Inference ($18.3$~ms per image for DGTS, about $75$~ms for DOTS on one T4 GPU) is feasible
at laboratory level, and fairness, deskilling and accountability remain open ethical
questions. The General Conclusion now brings together the contributions, limits and
perspectives of the thesis.
