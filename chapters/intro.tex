\section*{Context}

Diabetes is one of the fastest-growing chronic diseases in the world: it affects an
estimated 537 million adults, and this number is expected to reach 783 million by
2045~\cite{idf2021atlas}. Its most frequent complication in the eye, diabetic retinopathy
(DR), affects about 103 million people and is a leading cause of preventable blindness in
adults of working age~\cite{teo2021global,cheung2010dr}. The tragedy of DR is that it is
silent: vision is usually preserved until the disease is advanced, whereas treatment is
most effective in the early stages. Regular screening of the retina of every diabetic
patient is therefore the most effective way to prevent blindness at the scale of a population.

Screening relies on fundus photographs graded by trained experts on the five-level
International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) scale~\cite{wilkinson2003icdr}. The number of images to grade now far
exceeds the number of available graders, especially in countries where ophthalmologists
are scarce. Automated grading by deep learning has become the natural answer, and in recent years
backbones have steadily improved: in the reproductions of this thesis on Kaggle EyePACS,
the quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) rises from $0.840$ with EfficientNet-B3
(2019~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}) to $0.920$ with the retinal foundation model RETFound
(2023~\cite{zhou2023retfound}).

\paragraph{Scope of the term ``disease progression''.}
In this thesis, the term ``disease progression'' used in the title is operationalised
through the prediction of diabetic retinopathy severity and ordinal grading from medical
images: each fundus image is assigned a grade on the five-level ICDR scale, whose levels
correspond to successive stages of the disease. The present work is therefore primarily
cross-sectional: every patient contributes a single visit, and the thesis does not
predict the future trajectory of an individual patient. Longitudinal modelling of disease
progression, which requires repeated observations of the same patients over time, is
considered a future extension (see the General Conclusion).

\section*{Problem Statement}

Despite this progress, none of the representative systems reviewed here simultaneously
addresses the three architectural requirements defined in this thesis within a single
evaluated framework. Several systems have been validated prospectively and deployed
(\cref{ch:sota}); the point made here is architectural, not a statement about the whole
literature. Three limitations recur across the representative systems reviewed in this
thesis:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{L1 -- no relational context.} Each fundus image is graded in isolation, so
patients who look alike, and who often share a grade, never inform one another.
\item \textbf{L2 -- no vascular topology.} Convolutional and transformer networks see local
texture; they do not explicitly measure the global structure of the vascular tree (its connected
components and loops), which changes as the disease progresses.
\item \textbf{L3 -- no right to abstain.} Every image receives a grade, even when it is
ambiguous. The most difficult cases, at the boundary between moderate and severe disease,
are forced through, and the rate of severe cases that are not referred (Sev-NR) stays
above the $2\%$ evaluation target adopted in this thesis.
\end{itemize}
In the experiments of this thesis these limitations interact: the population graph and the
topological features each bring a partial gain, their combination brings the largest one,
and abstention reaches the Sev-NR target only when the
underlying predictions are accurate enough. This motivates addressing them together, in a
single architecture. \Cref{fig:map} summarises how each limitation leads to
a research question, to a component of the proposed system and to the result obtained in
this thesis.

\section*{Objectives and Value of the Work}

This thesis aims to design, retrospectively evaluate and engineer a DR grading system that
addresses the three limitations within a single architecture. More precisely, it aims to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item build an inductive population graph that lets similar patients share information,
while grading a new patient in constant time;
\item describe the retinal vascular network with persistent-homology features and use them
both as patient features and inside the similarity that builds the graph;
\item add ordinal prediction with guaranteed rank consistency and a calibrated abstention
mechanism, so that ambiguous cases are sent to a human grader;
\item evaluate each step with statistical tests fixed before the experiments, and design the
software according to regulatory-oriented principles relevant to medical-device software.
\end{itemize}

The work thus pursues a double ambition. On the scientific side, it shows that the three
limitations can be addressed together and that, within the investigated architectural
family and experimental specification, the complete relational--structural--ordinal
configuration is the only evaluated configuration whose Sev-NR point estimate falls below
the $2\%$ target while satisfying the complete set of predefined criteria. On the
practical side, it delivers a research system that reaches QWK $=0.951$ and a Sev-NR point
estimate of $1.9\%$ (95\% CI $[1.2\%,\,2.8\%]$) on retrospective public benchmarks, with a
measured latency of $31.4$~ms per image under the laboratory configuration of
\cref{app:repro}, and whose architecture was designed according to regulatory-oriented
principles relevant to IEC~62304, ISO~14971 and the EU AI Act. For an illustrative
programme of $500{,}000$ patients a year, the lower non-referral rate corresponds to a
\emph{projected} reduction of about $600$ potentially non-referred severe cases under the
explicit prevalence assumptions of \cref{sec:impact}; this is a projection, not an observed
clinical outcome.

The central claim of the thesis is deliberately bounded. The thesis does not claim that
DOTS is clinically validated or regulatory-ready; it demonstrates that, within the
investigated architectural family, relational, structural/topological and ordinal-safety
mechanisms can be integrated into a single system that achieves favourable
safety-oriented performance on retrospective public benchmarks.

\section*{Research Methodology}

The work follows a hypothesis-driven methodology. Three research hypotheses structure the
thesis: \textbf{H1}, a population graph adds information beyond that captured by a per-image
classifier; \textbf{H2}, persistent-homology features of the vessel network are
statistically associated with disease severity; and \textbf{H3}, the combination of the
three axes meets seven predefined clinical-oriented criteria (H3a) and, within the
investigated architectural family, no proper subset of the axes does (H3b). For each hypothesis, the statistical test, the decision threshold and the
effect-size measure were fixed before the corresponding experiment was run, so that a
negative result could not be explained away afterwards; the falsification criteria are
listed in \cref{tab:falsif}. Because the inferential unit of H1 is the cross-validation
fold, its paired $t$-test has only four degrees of freedom; bootstrap and exact permutation
analyses are therefore reported as complementary robustness checks rather than as
independent replications. No held-out test set was used to choose the backbone, the graph
parameters, the class weights, the abstention threshold, the final architecture or any
other hyperparameter; each test set was consulted once, after these choices were frozen
(\cref{sec:protocol}). The components were introduced progressively, from a generic
graph template evaluated outside medicine, to TOPORET, and finally to DOTS, so that the
contribution of each component can be measured separately. All experiments use public
datasets, fixed random seeds and released code (\cref{app:repro}), so that they can be
reproduced independently. Sample sizes are reported in retinal images: the $108{,}301$
images of the five benchmarks (\cref{tab:datasets}) are not necessarily independent
patients, so the results are image-level and patient-level generalisation is interpreted
with caution.

\section*{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis, supported by eleven publications (see the List of
Publications), are the following.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=sameline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[C1.] A graph template that augments node features with interpretable node-level
descriptors, evaluated outside medicine on microservice orchestration and on four
anomaly-detection datasets; its ten-run re-evaluation shows that the descriptors bring
interpretability and robustness to drift rather than a significant accuracy gain
\cite{belhadj2025icaart,belhadj2026invariants,belhadj2026lnaiinv}.
\item[C2.] Evidence, obtained with three independent methods, that topological and
structural features of the retina carry information that deep visual features miss,
including SAMF-GB, a CPU model more accurate than a GPU EfficientNet-B3 at about $1/13$ of
its cost \cite{belhadj2026hybrid,mezghich2026lnaitopo,belhadj2026icpram,belhadj2026samfgb}.
\item[C3.] TOPORET, an inductive dual-similarity population graph for DR grading
introduced in this thesis, with pre-specified tests supporting the contribution of the
graph (H1) and the association of the topological features with severity (H2) \cite{belhadj2025plos,belhadj2026enase,belhadj2026miua}.
\item[C4.] DOTS, a three-axis DR grading architecture combining relational,
structural/topological and ordinal-safety mechanisms. On the evaluated retrospective
benchmarks, DOTS reaches the predefined seven-criterion target at the point-estimate level
(H3a), while exhaustive evaluation of the six proper subsets shows that none reaches the
complete criterion set within the investigated architectural family (H3b)
\cite{belhadj2026dots}.
\item[C5.] A software architecture with typed, unit-tested stages, an audit trail and
attention-derived explanatory signals, together with a regulatory-oriented mapping of its
design to IEC~62304, ISO~14971 and the EU AI Act (not a conformity assessment).
\end{description}

\section*{Thesis Organisation}

The thesis is organised in six chapters, followed by a General
Conclusion that summarises the contributions, their limits and the perspectives they open:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Diabetic Retinopathy and Automated Grading} (\cref{ch:sota}): the disease,
its grading scale, screening programmes, two decades of methods for automated grading, and the limits
they share.
\item \textbf{Learning on Graphs, Topology and Ordinal Prediction}
(\cref{ch:background}): the methodological background -- graph neural networks,
persistent homology, ordinal regression, calibration and abstention -- and the evaluation
metrics.
\item \textbf{Structural and Topological Priors for Retinal Image Grading}
(\cref{ch:found}): evaluation of the graph template outside medicine, and first evidence
that structural features help DR grading.
\item \textbf{Topology-Aware Population Graph for Diabetic Retinopathy Grading}
(\cref{ch:toporet}): TOPORET, the dual-similarity population graph, and the tests of H1
and H2.
\item \textbf{Safety-Oriented Ordinal Grading of Diabetic Retinopathy with Abstention}
(\cref{ch:dots}): DOTS, the complete system, the seven-criterion evaluation (H3a), the
scoped necessity analysis (H3b), a projection of its clinical impact and its comparison
with the literature.
\item \textbf{Software Architecture and Regulatory-Oriented Design} (\cref{ch:reg}): how
the design of DOTS was shaped by regulatory-oriented principles for medical-device
software, and what this mapping does not establish.
\end{enumerate}

Readers with different backgrounds may follow different paths through the document.
Clinicians and regulatory readers will find a self-contained account in \cref{ch:sota},
\cref{ch:dots} and \cref{ch:reg}, together with the General Conclusion. Machine-learning
researchers will find the methodological background in \cref{ch:background} and the
technical contributions, with their algorithms and experiments, in
\cref{ch:found,ch:toporet,ch:dots}. Readers who wish to check the statistics or reproduce
the experiments will find the formal tests in \cref{app:stats} and the software
environment, seeds and computational cost in \cref{app:repro}.
