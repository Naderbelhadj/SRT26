\section*{Context}

Diabetes is one of the fastest-growing chronic diseases in the world: it affects an
estimated 537 million adults, and this number is expected to reach 783 million by
2045~\cite{idf2021atlas}. Its most frequent complication in the eye, diabetic retinopathy
(DR), affects about 103 million people and is a leading cause of preventable blindness in
adults of working age~\cite{teo2021global,cheung2010dr}. The tragedy of DR is that it is
silent: vision is usually preserved until the disease is advanced, whereas treatment is
most effective in the early stages. Regular screening of the retina of every diabetic
patient is therefore the most effective way to prevent blindness at the scale of a population.

Screening relies on fundus photographs graded by trained experts on the five-level
International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) scale~\cite{wilkinson2003icdr}. The number of images to grade now far
exceeds the number of available graders, especially in countries where ophthalmologists
are scarce. Automated grading by deep learning has become the natural answer: convolutional
networks now match human graders for the detection of referable
DR~\cite{gulshan2016jama,ting2017jama}, and backbones have steadily improved, from
compound-scaled networks such as EfficientNet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} to retinal
foundation models such as RETFound~\cite{zhou2023retfound}.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The task studied in this thesis is the prediction of disease severity: each fundus image
is assigned a grade on the five-level ICDR scale, whose levels correspond to successive
stages of the disease. The work is cross-sectional: every patient contributes a single
visit, and the thesis does not predict the future trajectory of an individual patient.
Longitudinal modelling, which requires repeated observations of the same patients over
time, is a future extension (see the General Conclusion).

\section*{Problem Statement}

Despite this progress, none of the representative systems reviewed here addresses the
three architectural requirements defined in this thesis. Several systems have been
validated prospectively and deployed (\cref{ch:sota}); the point made here is
architectural, not a statement about the whole literature. Three limitations recur across
the representative systems reviewed in this thesis:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{L1 -- no relational context.} Each fundus image is graded in isolation, so
patients who look alike, and who often share a grade, never inform one another.
\item \textbf{L2 -- no vascular topology.} Convolutional and transformer networks see local
texture; they do not explicitly measure the global structure of the vascular tree (its connected
components and loops), which changes as the disease progresses.
\item \textbf{L3 -- no safety-oriented grading.} The grades are treated as unordered
categories, so that confusing No DR with proliferative DR costs the same as confusing two
neighbouring grades, and the rate of severe cases that would not be referred (Sev-NR) is
rarely measured, let alone on data from another country.
\end{itemize}
In the experiments of this thesis these limitations interact: the population graph and the
topological features each bring a partial gain and their combination brings the largest
one, while a model that generalises well in accuracy does not necessarily meet a safety
threshold. This motivates studying them together, in one research programme.
\Cref{fig:map} summarises how each limitation leads to a research question, to a component
of the proposed systems and to the result obtained in this thesis.

Each limitation is defined formally in \cref{sec:limits}, and each hypothesis is tied to
one of them, so that the outcome of every test can be read as evidence on one limitation.

\section*{Objectives and Value of the Work}

This thesis aims to design and retrospectively evaluate DR grading systems that address
the three limitations, and to engineer them as verifiable software. More precisely, it
aims to:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item build an inductive population graph that lets similar patients share information,
while grading a new patient without retraining;
\item describe the retinal vascular network with persistent-homology features and use them
both as patient features and inside the similarity that builds the graph;
\item add ordinal prediction and evaluate it against screening safety criteria, internally
and on an external dataset;
\item evaluate each step with explicit statistical tests, and design the software according
to regulatory-oriented principles relevant to medical-device software.
\end{itemize}

The work thus pursues a double ambition. On the scientific side, it provides evidence that relational and topological information
improve DR grading on two different corpora, and that with ordinal supervision,
multi-source training and test-time augmentation the observed performance satisfies
pre-set screening safety criteria where none of the reduced variants does. On the practical
side, it delivers two research systems trained on $5{,}859$ public images. DGTS (Deep Graph
Topological System) reaches a quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) of $0.925$ without retraining on
$35{,}126$ EyePACS images. DOTS (Deep Ordinal Training System) reaches a QWK of $0.931$
and a Sev-NR of $1.4\%$ on the independent Messidor-2 dataset, with an inference time of
about $75$~ms per image on a single T4 GPU. In an illustrative
operational projection for a programme of $500{,}000$ patients a year, the lower
non-referral rate of DOTS corresponds to about $1{,}100$ fewer non-referred severe cases
than a CNN baseline, under the explicit prevalence assumptions of \cref{sec:impact}; this
calculation is not an estimate of clinical impact.

The central claim of the thesis is deliberately bounded. The thesis does not claim that
its systems are clinically validated or regulatory-ready, nor that the three axes have
been combined in a single model. It shows, on retrospective public data, that
relational, topological and ordinal-safety mechanisms each bring a measurable benefit to
DR grading, also observed on external data, within statistical limits stated with each
result.

\section*{Research Methodology}

The work follows a hypothesis-driven methodology. Three research hypotheses structure the
thesis: \textbf{H1}, a population graph adds information beyond that captured by a per-image
classifier; \textbf{H2}, persistent-homology features of the vessel network are
statistically associated with disease severity; and \textbf{H3}, ordinal supervision
within the DOTS pipeline satisfies pre-set screening safety criteria internally and on an external
dataset (H3a), whereas none of the reduced variants of its ablation does on the external
dataset (H3b). For H1 and H2 the statistical test, the decision threshold and the
effect-size measure were fixed before the corresponding experiment was run, so that a
negative result could not be explained away afterwards; the H3 criteria are evaluation
targets informed by screening practice, not regulatory thresholds. The falsification criteria are listed in
\cref{tab:falsif}. Because the inferential unit of H1 is the cross-validation fold, its
paired $t$-test has only four degrees of freedom; bootstrap, permutation and a correction
for the overlap of the folds are therefore reported with it, and the correction is
stated wherever it weakens the conclusion. External datasets were used only for evaluation, after the models were
trained (\cref{sec:protocol}). The components were introduced progressively, from a generic
graph template evaluated outside medicine, to TOPORET, and finally to DGTS and DOTS, so
that the contribution of each component can be measured separately. All experiments use
public datasets and fixed random seeds (\cref{app:repro}), so that they can be reproduced
independently. Sample sizes are reported in retinal images, which are not necessarily
independent patients, so the results are image-level and patient-level generalisation is
interpreted with caution.

\section*{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis, supported by thirteen publications, nine of them
accepted or published (see the List of
Publications), are the following.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=sameline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[C1.] A graph template that augments node features with interpretable node-level
descriptors, evaluated outside medicine on microservice orchestration and on four
anomaly-detection datasets; its ten-run re-evaluation shows that the descriptors bring
interpretability and robustness to drift rather than a significant accuracy gain
\cite{belhadj2025icaart,belhadj2026invariants,belhadj2026lnaiinv,belhadj2025ijcnn}.
\item[C2.] Evidence, obtained with three independent methods, that topological and
structural features of the retina carry information that deep visual features miss,
including SAMF-GB, a CPU model more accurate than a GPU EfficientNet-B3, whose
classifier is about $13$ times cheaper to run \cite{belhadj2026hybrid,mezghich2026lnaitopo,belhadj2026icpram,belhadj2026samfgb}.
\item[C3.] TOPORET, a dual-similarity population graph for DR grading
introduced in this thesis, with pre-specified tests that provide evidence supporting the contribution of the
graph (H1) and a statistical association of the topological features with severity (H2) \cite{belhadj2025plos,belhadj2026enase,belhadj2026miua}.
\item[C4.] Two systems trained on a fused multi-source corpus. DGTS is a population graph
regularised by persistent homology of feature-space neighbourhoods, validated without
retraining on $35{,}126$ EyePACS images \cite{belhadj2026dgts}. DOTS is an ordinal,
safety-oriented grading pipeline whose observed performance satisfies the pre-set
safety criteria at the point estimate internally and on Messidor-2, whereas none of its reduced variants does
externally (H3) \cite{belhadj2026dots}.
\item[C5.] A software architecture with six typed, testable stages, a risk analysis and an
audit-trail design, together with a regulatory-oriented mapping to IEC~62304, ISO~14971 and
the EU AI Act (not a conformity assessment).
\end{description}

\paragraph{Nature of the contributions.}
The contributions are of three kinds. The \emph{scientific} contributions are the
evidence obtained for relational information (C3, and DGTS in C4), the statistical
association of topological descriptors with severity (C2, C3), and the screening-oriented
evaluation of ordinal grading (DOTS in C4). The \emph{methodological} contributions are the
graph template with interpretable descriptors (C1), the dual-similarity graph (C3), the
feature-space topological regularisation and the ordinal pipeline (C4), and the
pre-specified evaluation protocol. The \emph{engineering} contributions are the modular
architecture, the released code and the regulatory-oriented mapping (C5).

\paragraph{Coherence of the contributions.}
TOPORET, DGTS and DOTS are three complementary contributions to a single objective:
exploiting the relations between images, the topology of the retinal structures and the
order of the grades to improve DR grading. The thesis proposes a three-axis research
framework rather than a single unified model: each axis is instantiated by a dedicated
system, so that its contribution can be studied separately. TOPORET tests the first two
ingredients together on three public benchmarks (L1, L2; H1, H2). DGTS carries them into
an inductive population graph regularised by feature-space persistent homology on a fused
multi-source corpus, and checks them on external data. DOTS, trained on the same corpus,
adds the third ingredient, ordinality, and evaluates it against screening safety criteria
(L3, H3). \Cref{tab:evidence} shows that the three systems provide different kinds of
evidence.

\section*{Thesis Organisation}

The thesis is organised in six chapters, followed by a General
Conclusion that summarises the contributions, their limits and the perspectives they open:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Diabetic Retinopathy and Automated Grading} (\cref{ch:sota}): the disease,
its grading scale, screening programmes, two decades of methods for automated grading, and the limits
they share.
\item \textbf{Learning on Graphs, Topology and Ordinal Prediction}
(\cref{ch:background}): the methodological background -- graph neural networks,
persistent homology, ordinal regression and calibration -- and the evaluation metrics.
\item \textbf{Structural and Topological Priors for Retinal Image Grading}
(\cref{ch:found}): evaluation of the graph template outside medicine, and first evidence
that structural features help DR grading.
\item \textbf{Topology-Aware Population Graph for Diabetic Retinopathy Grading}
(\cref{ch:toporet}): TOPORET, the dual-similarity population graph, and the tests of H1
and H2.
\item \textbf{Topology-Regularised Graphs and Safety-Oriented Ordinal Grading}
(\cref{ch:dots}): DGTS and DOTS on a multi-source corpus, their external validation, the
safety criteria (H3), an illustrative operational projection and a comparison of the two
systems.
\item \textbf{Software Architecture and Regulatory-Oriented Design} (\cref{ch:reg}): how
the design of the pipelines was shaped by regulatory-oriented principles for
medical-device software, and what this mapping does not establish.
\end{enumerate}

Readers with different backgrounds may follow different paths through the document.
Clinicians and regulatory readers will find a self-contained account in \cref{ch:sota},
\cref{ch:dots} and \cref{ch:reg}, together with the General Conclusion. Machine-learning
researchers will find the methodological background in \cref{ch:background} and the
technical contributions, with their algorithms and experiments, in
\cref{ch:found,ch:toporet,ch:dots}. Readers who wish to check the statistics or reproduce
the experiments will find the formal tests in \cref{app:stats} and the software
environment, seeds and computational cost in \cref{app:repro}.
